## Appendix A. Ethical Statement and Controlled Access Protocol

This research involves the generation and analysis of machine-generated fake news, which carries inherent risks. The MegaFake dataset includes deceptive content generated by Large Language Models (LLMs). While the primary objective of this work is to advance defensive technologies and understand the linguistic patterns of machine-generated disinformation, we acknowledge that the open dissemination of such datasets and the associated generation pipelines could be exploited by malicious actors to mass-produce disinformation.

Given these significant concerns, we have implemented a Controlled Access Framework to strictly regulate the distribution of the dataset and code. Unlike standard open-source releases, the MegaFake resources are not publicly downloadable. Instead, we have established a gated release protocol designed to ensure responsible use. Researchers requesting access must submit a formal application via our repository, which is subject to a rigorous review process by the project team.

To be granted access, applicants must fulfill the following three conditions:

1. **Institutional Verification**: Applicants must provide proof of affiliation with a recognized academic institution or research organization. Requests from anonymous or personal email addresses will be automatically rejected.
2. **Defensive Research Plan**: Applicants must submit a detailed statement outlining their intended use of the data. Access is restricted exclusively to research aimed at combating misinformation (e.g., detection modeling, linguistic analysis, or interpretability studies).
3. **Binding Data Use Agreement (DUA)**: Applicants must sign a legally binding agreement committing to: (a) use the data solely for the stated research purpose; (b) refrain from redistributing the dataset or code to third parties; and (c) abstain from using the generation pipeline to deploy content on social media platforms.

Furthermore, regarding the prompt engineering aspect, while we describe the methodology for scientific reproducibility, we have redacted specific "jailbreak" triggers from the public documentation to prevent direct replication of harmful generation by non-experts. We believe this framework strikes a necessary balance between facilitating defensive research and mitigating the societal risks of AI-generated disinformation.

## Appendix B. Dataset Construction Details

### Dataset Construction and Model Selection

We apply the LLM-Fake Theory within this pipeline to guide the generation process. We begin by sourcing the GossipCop and PolitiFact datasets from the FakeNewsNet data repository. To ensure data quality, we first remove articles that lack either a title or news content. We then address variations in article length by eliminating the shortest and longest 10% of the articles to standardize news content length. After these preprocessing steps, our refined GossipCop dataset includes 11,945 legitimate news articles and 3,784 fake news articles, while the refined PolitiFact dataset includes 4,556 legitimate news articles and 2,445 fake news articles.

For LLM selection, we choose the General Language Model (GLM) and Llama, both of which have showed superior performance compared to models like GPT across equivalent model sizes and datasets. During data generation, for the content manipulation news type, we utilize the GLM4 model instead of ChatGLM due to its superior ability to handle numerical attributes. For all other news types, we use the ChatGLM3 model, which consumes fewer resources and offers better operational efficiency. Besides, for the information blending and news summarization news types, we use the Neural Topic Model to match similar news articles. Lastly, for both machine-generated legitimate news types, we conduct an analysis to select the optimal temperature setting for LLMs to ensure the generated content is free from hallucinations. The temperature parameter controls the randomness of LLMs' outputs, with higher temperatures increase output diversity but also raise the risk of generating hallucinated or less accurate content, while lower temperatures produce more deterministic and focused results. We evaluate the impact of different temperature values on model performance to determine the most appropriate setting for our analysis.

### Topic Modeling for Information Blending

We conduct experiments with various numbers of topics to identify the optimal setting for our analysis. The topic numbers evaluated are 5, 20, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 175, 200, 250, 300, 500. After thorough evaluation, we determine that 200 topics (n_topic = 200) provide the best results for our topic model. For each topic, we calculate the document-to-topic probabilities, representing the likelihood of each document belonging to a particular topic, and assign each document to the topic with the highest probability. To ensure robust representation, we exclude topics containing fewer than five documents. Within the remaining topics, we separate fake and legitimate news articles, rank them by their respective probabilities, and discard any unmatched data.

### Topic Modeling for News Summarization

Similarly, we conduct experiments with various topic numbers to identify the optimal setting for our analysis. The topic numbers evaluated are 20, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 175, 200, 300, 400, 500. After thorough evaluation, we find that 300 (n_topic = 300) is the optimal number of topics. Using this topic model, we calculate the document-to-topic probabilities and assign each article to the topic with the highest probability. To ensure substantial content representation in each category, we eliminate any topics containing fewer than five articles. The remaining articles are then sorted by their probability scores and divided into two groups based on odd and even indices, with any remaining items discarded.

### Temperature Selection for Writing Enhancement

We select a subset of 1,000 articles from an initial pool of 11,945 human-generated legitimate news articles and test various temperature settings: 0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3. We evaluate the risk of hallucination for each temperature setting using the ROUGE metric, which measures n-gram overlap between the LLM-generated and original texts. Lower ROUGE scores may indicate a higher risk of hallucination due to reduced content overlap. Our analysis shows that a temperature setting of 0.15 consistently yields the highest ROUGE scores, suggesting an optimal balance between output diversity and factual accuracy. Based on these results, we select a temperature of 0.15 (t = 0.15) for the final generation of our writing-enhanced machine-generated legitimate news articles.

### Dataset Statistics

We analyze a range of linguistic features, including the average number of sentences per text (Sent Count), the average number of words per text (Word Count), the average sentence length in words, reflecting syntactic complexity (Sent Len (Words)), the average sentence length in characters, indicating expression density (Sent Len (Chars)), and the average word length, which serves as an indicator of lexical complexity (Word Len). In addition, sentiment features are computed using VADER, an open-source Python package specifically attuned to sentiments expressed in social media. These features include the overall sentiment score (Polarity, ranging from -1 to +1, with negative values denoting negative sentiment) and its standard deviation (Polarity Std, reflecting sentiment consistency).

Average sentence and word counts are generally higher in PolitiFact than in GossipCop. Similarly, sentence lengths, measured in both words and characters, tend to be slightly greater in PolitiFact. Across all conditions, average word length remains consistent, ranging from 4.04 to 5.01 characters, indicating stable lexical complexity regardless of model or dataset. Narrative generation and content manipulation news types typically exhibit higher word counts and longer sentences, reflecting more elaborate narratives compared to sheep's clothing or information blending news types. Sentiment scores are generally positive across most categories, with only slight variations, while sentiment standard deviations are moderate (ranging from 0.50 to 0.88), indicating some variability in sentiment within categories. Overall, the differences between GLM and Llama are relatively minor in terms of dataset statistics, suggesting a high degree of consistency in the generated datasets across models.

## Appendix C. Dataset Quality Evaluation

### Hallucination Analysis

Given that our focus is on the intentional generation of fake news using LLMs, aligning with the deceptive motives defined in the literature, we explore the potential impact of LLM hallucination. To this end, we apply PURIFY, an LLM hallucination detection method that evaluates logical fidelity, factual integrity, semantic consistency, and contextual alignment between the original human-written text and its synthetic LLM-generated counterpart. The author report that PURIFY detects hallucinated misalignments in 38% of the data generated by GPT-3.5-turbo. While most content produced by GPT-3.5-turbo is contextually and semantically aligned, 8% of the overall samples still show logical misalignment, and a further 30% exhibit factual inconsistencies.

We apply PURIFY to evaluate two types of fake news, i.e., sheep's clothing and information blending, as well as all legitimate news types in MegaFake. We find that 9.23% of the data generated by GLM and Llama contains hallucinated misalignments. While most generated samples are contextually and semantically aligned, only 1.05% of the overall samples demonstrate logical misalignment, and 11.37% exhibit factual inconsistencies likely due to hallucinations. These results give us some confidence that the deceptive features reflected in MegaFake are likely guided by our LLM-Fake Theory and the strong implicit reasoning abilities of LLMs, rather than being artifacts of LLM hallucinations. Although hallucination in LLMs is currently unavoidable and cannot be entirely eliminated by existing method, we acknowledge this as a limitation and recognize it as an inherent characteristic of LLM-generated content.

## Appendix D. Human Evaluation

We recruit two groups of annotators to assess the quality of the generated content. First, we randomly sample 1% of the MegaFake dataset from each news type, resulting in 1,708 samples. Fifty human annotators are then recruited via social media platforms to independently evaluate the quality of each sample. Additionally, we engage with ten university students to conduct a more in-depth review, focusing on both the quality and appropriateness of the content. These students receive more instructions and background knowledge on evaluating fake news. To ensure the reliability of our human evaluation, we calculate inter-annotator agreement using Cohen’s kappa, quantifying the consistency among annotators.

The results of our human evaluation demonstrate that MegaFake exhibits strong overall quality. Human annotators rate 96.14% of randomly sampled articles as fluent and contextually relevant, and 98.72% of machine-generated legitimate news as free from toxic or biased language. Among university student annotators, 89.16% of the evaluated samples are confirmed to be of high quality and appropriate. The inter-annotator agreement, measured by Cohen’s kappa, is 0.82 on average, indicating substantial consistency among annotators’ judgments. These results suggest that the dataset maintains a high standard of quality. The detailed questions used for human evaluation are provided below:
